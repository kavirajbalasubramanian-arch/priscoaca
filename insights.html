Study notes

Guidelines first, code books second

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

New coders often open the code book first and the guidelines later. Experienced coders do it the other way around, and the difference shows in their accuracy.

Why the guideline comes first

Code descriptors tell you what a code means. Guidelines tell you when you are allowed to use it, in what order, and with what else. Two coders can find the same code and still disagree, because only one of them checked the rule that governs it.

A simple routine

  • Read the documentation once for the story, then again for the details.
  • Identify the main diagnosis or procedure and the supporting detail.
  • Check the relevant chapter or section guidelines before selecting a code.
  • Confirm the code in the book, and check notes such as excludes, use additional code and code first.
  • Ask whether the documentation supports it, line by line.
The code book answers “what.” The guideline answers “may I.”

Build this routine in practice and it becomes automatic by exam day, and by your first week on a live queue.

CPC or CCS: choosing your first credential

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

Both credentials are well regarded, and both open doors. They tend to point towards different kinds of work.

CPC, from AAPC

The Certified Professional Coder credential is commonly associated with physician-office and outpatient professional coding, where claims are built from the provider’s documentation of a visit or procedure.

CCS, from AHIMA

The Certified Coding Specialist credential is commonly associated with hospital and facility coding, including inpatient records, where a longer stay is summarized into a single set of codes.

How to choose

  • Think about the setting you want to work in, not only the exam.
  • Look at job descriptions in your target role and note which credential they ask for.
  • Speak to a trainer about your background and goals.
  • Remember that many professionals earn both over time.

Certification exams are set and awarded by AAPC and AHIMA. Our programmes prepare you for them; we are not the certifying body.

Reading a chart like a coder

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

A medical record can look like a wall of text. Coders learn to read it with purpose.

Start with the reason for the encounter

Why did the patient come in, and what did the provider conclude? The assessment and plan usually hold the most useful summary, but the rest of the note shows whether the conclusions are supported.

Then separate three things

  • Diagnoses that the provider documented and that were addressed or relevant.
  • Procedures and services that were performed, and how they were performed.
  • Support: the findings, results and decisions that justify each code.

Know what is missing

Sometimes the most important skill is noticing a gap: a diagnosis that is implied but not stated, or a procedure with no detail. In those moments a coder does not guess. A compliant query to the provider asks for clarification.

Code what is documented, not what is assumed.

E/M levels, explained simply

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

Evaluation and management (E/M) codes describe the work of a visit. Selecting the right level is one of the most common tasks a coder performs.

Two ways to choose a level

For most E/M categories, the level is selected by medical decision making or, where the category allows it, by total time on the date of the encounter. Always confirm the rules for the specific category and the year in the current guidelines.

What medical decision making looks at

  • The number and complexity of problems addressed
  • The amount and complexity of data reviewed and analyzed
  • The risk of complications, morbidity or mortality from management

A method that works

Read the note, list the problems addressed, list the data, identify the management and its risk, and then match the result to the level definitions. Write down your reasoning, because in an audit it is the reasoning that gets defended.

Practice on de-identified charts until the steps feel natural. Speed follows clarity.

How to practise with mock exams

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

A mock exam is a rehearsal, not a verdict. Used well, it shows you exactly where to spend your next hour of study.

Before the mock

  • Set up your code books and tabs the way you will on exam day.
  • Work in one sitting, with a timer, and without interruptions.

During the mock

  • Mark questions you are unsure about, and move on rather than stall.
  • Practise navigating the books quickly; finding a code is a skill of its own.

After the mock

Keep an error log. For every miss, write the topic, the reason you missed it (knowledge, guideline, reading, or time) and the fix. Within a few mocks, patterns appear, and your revision becomes targeted rather than general.

The score tells you where you are. The error log tells you where to go.
Career

From classroom to first coding role

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

Finishing a course is a milestone, not the finish line. Employers look for people who can show they are ready for real work.

Show your readiness

  • Keep a record of practice charts and the reasoning behind your code choices.
  • Be able to explain a guideline, not only recite a code.
  • Learn the vocabulary of production and quality: queues, turnaround, audits and feedback.

Prepare the paperwork

A clear, honest resume that lists your training, your credential status and the specialties you practised. Avoid overstating; a precise resume signals a precise coder.

Prepare for the interview

Expect questions on guidelines, documentation gaps and how you handle feedback. Practise out loud with a trainer, and prepare examples of how you corrected a mistake.

Results depend on your effort, your eligibility and employer openings. Our placement support helps with each step, from resume to interview.

Your first 90 days learning medical coding

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

Coding rewards steady practice more than long cramming sessions. Here is a realistic plan for a beginner studying part-time.

Days 1 to 30: the language of medicine

  • Learn word roots, prefixes and suffixes until you can break down unfamiliar terms.
  • Cover the body systems one at a time, with the common conditions of each.
  • Read how a US healthcare claim moves from visit to payment.

Days 31 to 60: your first code sets

  • Learn how to use the ICD-10-CM index and tabular list, then the official guidelines.
  • Start CPT with evaluation and management, and learn how a level is chosen.
  • Code ten short practice notes every week and write down why you chose each code.

Days 61 to 90: put it together

  • Add HCPCS Level II and the most common modifiers.
  • Practise on full charts with several diagnoses and procedures.
  • Take a timed mock test, list your weak areas and revise them before the next one.

Use the readiness tracker every Sunday to decide what to study next week.

What does a medical coder do all day?

By the PRISCO MedCode Academy editorial desk

A coder turns what a clinician wrote into the codes a payer understands. The work is quiet, detail-heavy and more investigative than most people expect.

The daily loop

  • Open a chart. Read the visit note, orders and results.
  • Find the story. What was the reason for the visit, what was found and what was done?
  • Choose codes. Select diagnosis and procedure codes using the guidelines, not memory.
  • Check the edits. Make sure code pairs, modifiers and units follow the rules.
  • Raise a query. If the note is unclear, ask the provider instead of guessing.
  • Submit and learn. Review auditor feedback and denials to improve next time.

Skills that matter

Careful reading, comfort with code books and encoders, patience and honesty. Good coders code what the record supports, no more and no less.
